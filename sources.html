Image sources & rendering

The scene is rendered in real time from photographic and satellite-derived texture maps. These are historical composites, not live views. Cloud movement, camera positions and lighting are illustrative.

Cities, satellite imagery & current weather

The city explorer includes Abu Dhabi, Stockholm, Tokyo, New York, Quito, Birmingham in the United Kingdom, Wellington in New Zealand and Melbourne in Australia. Coordinates identify representative city centres, rather than administrative boundaries or the visitor's location. The globe approach is an illustrative camera transition; the city map is a separate geographic view.

Embedded city imagery, when configured, comes from the official Google Maps JavaScript API satellite map, with Google's and its imagery providers' attribution retained in the map. Satellite/aerial coverage combines imagery captured at different times; it is not live and does not show the weather reported below it. Google Earth images are not scraped or repurposed as globe textures. See Google Maps attribution and use policies.

Without the configured browser key, no embedded satellite imagery is loaded. The city-specific external link uses Google's documented, keyless Maps URL: https://www.google.com/maps/@?api=1&map_action=map&center=LAT%2CLON&zoom=15&basemap=satellite. The same link remains available if the embedded service fails.

Current weather is supplied by Open-Meteo, with data licensed under CC BY 4.0. Its current-conditions documentation describes 15-minutely weather-model data, not a direct live weather-station observation. The displayed update time comes from the provider's Unix timestamp and is formatted in the selected city's IANA time zone. Temperature, apparent temperature, wind and humidity describe that model update; units are °C, km/h and percent.

The city panel's real civil clock and current weather are independent of the globe's illustrative solar-time slider. While active and visible, the panel checks for fresh data every ten minutes. Recent responses are cached for the session; an older update or a failed refresh is identified rather than presented as newly fetched conditions. Refresh retries the request. The city selection supplies the coordinates; no visitor geolocation permission is requested.

The free Open-Meteo endpoint needs no API key for noncommercial use. Its service terms require fewer than 10,000 calls per day, 5,000 per hour and 600 per minute. Commercial deployments need the appropriate subscription and endpoint; these service limits are separate from the data's attribution licence.

Each city’s “Imagine that” stories are original short adaptations, with a source beside every story. Sources include Te Ara (Wellington’s wind), UNESCO (Quito), the US National Park Service (New York), Canal & River Trust (Birmingham and the wider Black Country network), Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria (Melbourne), the Vasa Museum (Stockholm), Tokyo Skytree and Louvre Abu Dhabi. Pages were checked on 30 October 2026. Wellington is described as famously windy, without claiming an unqualified global city record. Weather symbols are original SVG illustrations, not photographs or observed clouds.

Beneath Venus’s clouds

NASA/JPL’s Cylindrical Map of Venus combines Magellan synthetic-aperture radar observations from its 1990–1994 mission. The 3840 × 1920 JPEG is an unchanged Wikimedia Commons thumbnail of the global map. Both source pages identify this NASA/JPL image as public domain; see the JPL image-use policy.

This is a false-colour radar reconstruction. Its orange hues are simulated, informed by Venera 13 and 14 photographs; they are not the visible-light appearance of the whole surface. Magellan mapped about 98% of Venus. Gaps in this mosaic use Pioneer-Venus Orbiter altimetry or a constant mid-range value. Mosaic seams and filled patches are preserved. The map is equirectangular, with north at the top and both edges at 240° east longitude.

The moving marker is an artificial teaching aid for retrograde rotation, not an observed surface feature or calibrated longitude marker. Animation speed and globe lighting are illustrative. Exact download, provenance, limitations and image hash.

Twelve moons

Earth’s Moon, Jupiter’s four Galilean moons, and seven major moons of Saturn have individual close-ups and three explorable moon-system views. Maps are historical spacecraft composites with uneven coverage and resolution. The Moon uses a 4096 × 2048 JPEG; other moons use 2048 × 1024 derivatives with Lanczos resizing.

Six icy Saturn moons use Cassini mosaics made with enhanced ultraviolet/infrared colours. The close-up renderer reduces their saturation to 25% for a restrained appearance; this is an artistic treatment, not a calibrated true-colour conversion. Europa and Callisto use monochrome USGS mosaics. Around 4% of each source has missing coverage: these small gaps are smoothly interpolated without invented geological detail. Coverage masks and exact processing notes are retained with the assets.

Titan begins with a representative haze map. The illuminated atmospheric edge is an illustrative lighting effect, not a measured layer thickness. “Surface reconstruction” shows a map assembled from infrared observations, with polar lakes drawn from radar mosaics and colours informed by Huygens. It is explicitly not an unobstructed visible-light photograph. “Find the northern lakes” tilts the model toward its north pole and changes the illustrative lighting; it is not a spacecraft observation or a calibrated geographic measurement.

Earth–Moon lighting

The Earth–Moon view uses the same fixed sunlight direction for both bodies. Its 2048 × 1024 Earth and cloud maps are derivatives of the sources credited above. Cloud brightness supplies opacity without a colour-space conversion. Ocean reflectance and the thin, sunlit atmospheric edge are illustrative material effects, not measured roughness or atmosphere thickness. A faint neutral fill keeps the dark silhouettes readable. This view does not model eclipses, changing seasons or the Sun’s movement over a lunar month; its orbital clock is not a calibrated lunar-phase calendar. Earth’s daily rotation is omitted.

The same-face observation uses the model Moon’s actual local −X direction, transformed with its tidally locked mesh. Its gold pointer is a drawn orientation cue, not an observed surface mark, calibrated prime meridian, or light ray. Quarter-, half-, and full-orbit checkpoints are absolute fractions of the measured sidereal period from model Day 0; they are not lunar-phase dates. The Moon turns once in each orbit. Earth and Moon retain their diameter ratio while orbital distance is compressed.

Ocean tides

The interactive global ocean follows the Moon and Sun’s differential gravitational pull using quadrupole terms, P₂(n·direction). At mean distances, the Moon’s forcing has weight 1 and the Sun’s has weight 0.46. The model holds the Sun at its mean distance; a closer-Moon comparison changes the lunar weight with inverse distance cubed. See NASA: Tides and NOAA: tide-generating forces and spring/neap tides.

The gauge compares rise-and-fall range in the Moon–Sun plane, using the orbital plane when they align. At mean Moon distance, relative to Moon-only range 1, alignment gives 1.46 and a right angle gives 0.54. Both same-side and opposite-side alignments reinforce tides: spring tides occur around new and full Moon. “Spring” here is unrelated to the season. Neap tides have a smaller range around the quarter-Moon arrangements. These are ideal model ratios, not coastal measurements. The Sun switch briefly blends its contribution for visual continuity; that transition does not model the response time of real tides.

Water height is greatly exaggerated. The Moon’s displayed distance is compressed for composition. That display compression does not set its forcing; the closer-Moon comparison supplies a separate normalized distance to the tidal calculation. The pale line on the 3D globe marks Earth’s solid surface; it is not a measured coastline or mean sea-level contour. Water colour, transparency and edge lighting are enhanced so both bulges remain visible against space; these optical choices do not change the tide calculation. The global tide calculation keeps the Sun direction fixed and omits Earth’s daily spin, tidal lag and the effects of coasts, ocean basins and weather. It does not conserve fluid volume or predict local tide heights and times. Actual tides depend on local observations and geography: NOAA: why observed tides differ from the equilibrium model.

Spring tides and a “king tide” example

“King tide” is an informal name for an unusually high tide, not a separate scientific tide category or a universal height threshold. This exhibit shows one example: Sun–Moon alignment with a closer Moon, as in a perigean spring tide. Both the Sun and Moon contribute in all three comparisons. Earth’s changing distance from the Sun can also affect real tides, but this model holds that distance fixed. See the California Coastal Commission’s king-tide explanation and NOAA’s local high-tide flooding outlook.

The example places the Moon 5% closer than the model’s mean distance: lunar forcing becomes 1 ÷ 0.95³ ≈ 1.16635, giving an aligned range of about 1.62635. This illustrative distance is not an exact perigee, date or forecast. NASA describes the Moon’s varying perigee distance. The model holds each selected distance on a circular display path; it does not simulate an elliptical lunar orbit. The globe, illustrated guide and beach share the same forcing. Beach presets pause at the same high-water phase so their heights can be compared at a fixed scale. Local geography and weather affect actual water levels; storm surge is an additional weather effect, not the astronomical king tide itself. See NOAA: local tide heights and weather.

A separate day at the shore

The coastal scene connects that global range to an illustrative daily water-level change. Its own “Watch a day” clock turns a schematic coast marker through two ideal highs and two lows; it does not advance the Moon’s orbital clock. The Moon’s roughly 27-day arrangement is held when the visitor enters the coastal view. Moving the Moon or changing the Sun’s pull changes the size of the coastal variation, while the separate day slider selects its phase.

One displayed tide day represents approximately 24 hours 50 minutes: the mean lunar day, defined by rotation relative to the Moon, rather than a 24-hour solar day. NOAA’s glossary gives approximately 24.84 solar hours. The coast uses a simple two-cycle sinusoid with amplitude proportional to the shared model range, at a held alignment; it does not combine changing lunar and solar daily frequencies. It is an exaggerated teaching diagram without a real location, calibrated water-height units, coast-specific lag or hydrodynamic response. Real shores can have different daily tide patterns, unequal highs and lows, and different timings. See NOAA: rotation through the ideal tidal bulges and the equilibrium-model limits above.

Small waves, reflections, foam and wet sand are visual approximations, separate from the tide calculation. Neither the terrain nor its materials are a photograph or survey of a real beach. Moon-system motion

Moon radii follow JPL satellite physical parameters. Periods and orbital distances follow NASA’s Jovian satellite fact sheet, Saturnian satellite fact sheet and JPL satellite elements. One clock advances the moons at their measured relative rates. Paths are circular approximations with composed spacing. Most paths share a plane; the Moon is inclined about 5° and Iapetus about 15.5° to their reference planes. Orbital phases are chosen for clarity, not today’s positions. We omit eccentricity, precession, mutual perturbations and eclipses. Spins are synchronous; prime-meridian orientations are illustrative. Bodies are enlarged separately from distances. In the Earth–Moon scene, their diameter ratio is preserved; other moon systems enlarge small moons independently for visibility.

The asteroid belt

The belt entry is an imagined close-up flight, with generated cratered shapes, fractured faces, layered mineral shading and finer geometry for close passes. Surface references include NASA’s Bennu imagery. These are illustrations, not observed individual asteroids. Objects are much closer together than in the real belt; the forward speed is an artistic choice, not a spacecraft trajectory. Rocks approach, pass outside the camera, and are recycled only behind it. Individual spin axes and rates are illustrative. Occasional glancing contacts release chips and a brief dust veil; these are slowed, staged moments, not a collision simulation or a prediction of impact frequency. Real impacts can be far more energetic. See NASA’s observations of possible impact debris. Pause, speed and reduced-motion controls apply to this view. NASA’s Dawn mission FAQ explains the enormous real distances between asteroids.

The orrery shows 1,050 illustrative, enlarged samples in the main belt, about 2.1–3.3 AU from the Sun. These are generated paths, not tracked asteroids. Their orbital periods follow the approximate Kepler relation P² = a³. The belt has no hard edges, and real distances between objects are enormous. Composed view compresses the band; True spacing restores its approximate distance scale. The stories about sparse space, Ceres and Vesta link to NASA mission pages in the moon and belt research file.

How the orrery works

Positions use fixed J2000 elliptical elements from NASA/JPL: Approximate Positions of the Planets. Mean anomaly advances with each sidereal period; Kepler's equation determines changing speed along the ellipse. Inclination, ascending node and perihelion orientation are included. Starting phases are offset 16,550 days from J2000 for a readable composition. Element rates and perturbations are omitted, so this is an illustrative model, never a current ephemeris.

Composed view gives each orbit a chosen semi-major radius. True spacing uses one distance scale for every orbit, retaining their ratios and eccentricities. The Sun and planets remain independently enlarged as navigational markers in both modes. They are not a diameter comparison. Planet spin in this view is illustrative; relative orbital periods are modelled. The selected trail shows travel since the experiment began, up to one lap. The year experiment stops at 365.256 Earth days. Earth represents the Earth–Moon barycentre in the solar-system view; moons have separate neighbourhood views. The comparison counts completed whole trips and shows progress through the next one as a circle, using one consistent unit.

Orrery maps are 2048 by 1024 JPEG derivatives of the credited close-up maps, made with Lanczos resampling. Orrery lighting comes from the Sun; close-up lighting is chosen for exploration. The Sun's rendered texture and glow are illustrations. Three.js r180 is bundled locally under the MIT license.

Limits of the model

The maps represent different dates. Lighting and exposure are chosen for exploration. Earth has one fixed Sun declination and no changing seasons; local solar time uses longitude rather than civil time zones. Planet sizes are framed independently, except for the explicit Earth/Jupiter comparison. Cloud altitude, shadows and atmospheric scattering are approximations. This is a real-time browser reconstruction, not an offline film render or a fluid simulation.
