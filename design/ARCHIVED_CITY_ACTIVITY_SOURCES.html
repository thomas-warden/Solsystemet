City experiments: sources and models

Every city has an original interactive illustration; Wellington also has a fault experiment. These experiments are separate from the satellite map and current weather. Only Cape Town and Cook Strait use sampled elevation data. The other scenes are authored explanatory landscapes or cutaways, not measured local terrain. Motion, particles, lighting and most numerical relationships are simplified to reveal a mechanism; none predicts local wind, flooding, earthquakes, eruptions or erosion.

Terrain credits. Cape Town and Wellington elevation grids are resampled from Mapzen terrain tiles on AWS Open Data. Global GMTED2010 and SRTM terrain data courtesy of the U.S. Geological Survey; global ETOPO1 terrain data: U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Grids are resampled to 193 × 193, heights exaggerated, and surface materials procedurally illustrated. Exact source tile URLs are retained in Cape Town’s grid and Wellington’s grid. See the terrain provider attribution record. The small Earth texture uses the NASA-derived image already credited above. All other activity geometry, texture patterns, particles and interface graphics are original illustrations.

PlaceRelationship explored and limits
WellingtonCook Strait channels wind. Prescribed streamlines illustrate alignment and funneling; this is not a fluid-dynamics calculation or a measured speed map. The 1855 earthquake raised Wellington’s coast. The fault cutaway shows an illustrative uplift, not the geometry of that fault or a rupture threshold. The visitor triggers slip; no earthquake timing is inferred.
Cape TownOrographic cloud. Air starts at an illustrative 20°C; the control changes dew point from 0 to 19°C. Cloud base uses the approximation 125 × (temperature − dew point) metres. Condensation is drawn over terrain above that level. Actual cloud formation also depends on atmospheric stability, wind and moisture profiles.
Abu DhabiMangrove roots slow water and help sediment accumulate. Coverage controls a qualitative flow/settling illustration; tree growth and sediment budgets are not simulated.
StockholmPostglacial rebound. Ice removal and an asymptotic rise illustrate delayed response. The slider compresses geological time and does not encode a measured uplift rate.
TokyoUrban heat storage. Two illustrative exponential cooling curves colour paved and leafy neighbourhoods. They are not measured Tokyo temperatures or a forecast.
New YorkHudson tidal-current reversal. Sinusoidal flow provides flood, ebb and slack phases. Real current timing, freshwater flow and tidal lag vary along the estuary; the illustration does not represent today’s tide.
QuitoEarth’s tilt and seasonal daylight. Declination varies sinusoidally through ±23.44°. Geometric day length is 24/π × acos(−tan(latitude) × tan(declination)), comparing Quito and Stockholm. Refraction, terrain horizons and orbital eccentricity are omitted.
BirminghamConnected river drainage. An authored valley directs rain downhill into one stream. It is not a mapped Rea flood basin or a rainfall/runoff model.
MelbourneClay flocculation where fresh and salty water mix. Larger drawn clumps settle farther/faster as salt is added. Grain sizes and settling rates are qualitative, not calibrated measurements.
NairobiHighland climate. The thermometer samples an illustrative atmosphere with 30°C at sea level and the standard environmental lapse rate of 6.5°C/km. This is a comparison of ambient air at different elevations, not the dry adiabatic cooling rate of one sealed parcel or actual weather.
CairoDelta formation by sediment deposition. Authored deposition shapes split channels as land grows. This shows a formation mechanism over compressed time. Modern dams retain much of the Nile’s sediment, and parts of the present delta erode; the scene is not a claim that today’s delta is uniformly growing.
Rio de JaneiroDifferential erosion. A resistant dome remains as surrounding material lowers. Geometry and rates are illustrative; this is not a reconstruction of Sugarloaf’s full geological history.
AmsterdamPeat drainage, consolidation and oxidation. Lowering the water table is coupled to an exaggerated, compressed-time surface drop. Groundwater flow and real subsidence rates are not calculated.
CopenhagenStratified exchange in the Øresund. Increasing the density contrast reveals opposite surface and lower flows. Real circulation varies with wind, sea levels and mixing; the two-layer drawing is qualitative.
AucklandA volcanic field with multiple vents. A cutaway makes alternate magma paths visible. The visitor chooses a path; neither the depicted fractures nor the vents predict a future eruption.
