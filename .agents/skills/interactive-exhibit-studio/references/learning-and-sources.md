# Learning and sources

Give the visitor something worth noticing, a meaningful way to act, and an understandable consequence. Use factual and learning rigor to strengthen the exhibit without turning every experience into a sequence of cards and quizzes.

## Build learning into the exhibit

Define a small number of observable goals appropriate to the subject: compare size, trace a mechanism, inspect evidence, change a condition, or connect a pattern to a property. Let the signature interaction do this work.

For each substantial interaction, specify:

- What the visitor can change.
- What visibly changes in response.
- What they can infer from the result.
- What evidence or model supports that inference.
- What simplification or uncertainty matters to interpreting it.

Use guided activities when they help visitors discover the exhibit's possibilities. Choose their count and form from the experience; three missions and badges are optional product choices. Completion should depend on deliberate input or an evidence-based observation. Ambient motion, time spent on a page, or loading completion must not award a discovery.

Make feedback describe the actual observation. Use defensible tolerances instead of an arbitrary exact slider value. Accept multiple valid interpretations when the evidence permits them, and give a useful clue after a wrong choice. A completion badge records participation or accomplishment, not proven mastery.

## Keep age 8–10 instructions clear

Begin with a short, concrete invitation: “Turn the wheel. Watch the smaller gear.” Put the control beside the result and define necessary vocabulary near the thing it describes. Show a short explanation after the action; place extended reading and source detail where they can be explored without delaying the first useful interaction.

Preserve sophisticated imagery and composition. Child accessibility comes from legibility, understandable controls, observable consequences, and supportive feedback; it does not require a preschool visual style.

For emerging readers, offer fewer simultaneous choices, pictorial comparisons, and optional replayable audio with equivalent text. For older learners, reveal measurements, competing evidence, or model assumptions. Adapt to observed ability and language needs rather than age alone. Do not add narration unless it has a defined purpose and appropriate controls.

Start suitable scenes with gentle motion and an obvious pause. Honor reduced-motion preferences initially and when they change. Preserve keyboard operation, visible focus, large touch targets, and a useful alternative when graphics are unavailable. Avoid timed answers or a moving target that escapes keyboard focus.

## Keep claims traceable

Record important claims and measurements with source IDs, supporting pages or records, publisher or creator, relevant dates, units, conditions, and uncertainty. Inspect the actual source; an institution's homepage or a search snippet is not evidence for a specific claim. Keep asset provenance and reuse terms as described in [asset production](asset-production.md).

Distinguish representations where visitors encounter them:

- **Observation:** a photograph, specimen, measurement, or documented account.
- **Reconstruction:** an evidence-informed depiction with inferred parts.
- **Illustration:** an explanatory image that may simplify or alter scale.
- **Model:** a specified relationship or simulation with material assumptions.

Use concise language such as “Illustration; distances are compressed” or “Evidence suggests an ocean beneath the ice.” Explain caveats that change the meaning of the experience near the relevant view; keep technical implementation detail in supporting notes. Generated imagery and plausible animation cannot establish a claim.

## Adapt the learning to the subject

| Subject | Promising exhibit interaction | Evidence and interpretation limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ocean | Move through a depth transect, inspect a habitat, or compare how conditions change. | Identify location and conditions; light, temperature, pressure, salinity, and species distribution vary. Avoid a universal exact depth boundary or a deterministic ecosystem forecast without support. |
| Dinosaurs | Compare sourced size ranges, inspect a fossil feature, or reveal preserved and reconstructed anatomy. | Identify taxa, specimens, place, and period. Treat uncertain color, soft tissue, and behavior accordingly. Birds are living dinosaurs; distinguish non-avian extinction. |
| Cultures | Inspect an attributed object, trace a documented technique, or explore accounts tied to a place and time. | Use named creators and community sources; distinguish curatorial interpretation from lived accounts. Show variation and contemporary life. Avoid national stereotypes, invented traditions, or presenting restricted material as freely reusable. |
| Machines | Trace connected parts, change a lever ratio, inspect a cutaway, or compare documented designs. | Check the physical relationship, units, friction assumptions, and historical version. Mechanical advantage does not create energy. Separate documented use, patent claims, reconstruction, and disputed invention histories. |
| Periodic table | Explore families, change a particle count, or compare sourced material properties under stated conditions. | Distinguish atomic number, isotope, ion, and atomic weight; element, compound, and object. Properties depend on form and conditions. A diagram of electrons is a model, not a literal orbit photograph. |

Use trusted subject institutions, primary records where useful, and appropriately qualified explanations. For cultural exhibits, include community perspectives and creator attribution rather than relying only on external institutions. Choose the actual source for the claim at hand; a prestigious name alone is insufficient.

## Report what the work establishes

Separate factual review, functional tests, accessibility checks, visual critique, physical-device measurements, and observed learner sessions. Each supports different conclusions. Do not claim child testing, engagement, educational effectiveness, or learning gains unless those outcomes were actually investigated and recorded with their limits.

When evidence is missing, narrow the claim, label the reconstruction, choose another example, or document the unresolved limitation. Continue building the coherent, supported parts of the exhibit rather than inventing certainty.
