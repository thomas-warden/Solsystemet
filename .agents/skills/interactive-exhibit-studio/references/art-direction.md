# Art direction

Define a specific exhibit before choosing a page template. The first screen should make the subject recognizable and invite an action worth taking. For ages 8–10, make the invitation and controls clear while preserving the richness of the imagery and design.

## Establish the visual direction

Inspect actual references: the existing application, supplied material, relevant public exhibits, source photographs, museum collections, or the bundled reference. Open the images or rendered pages; a title or search snippet does not establish what they look like. Existing files and accessible public references are sufficient to begin; user uploads are optional.

Write a short direction note containing:

- **Experience:** what the visitor will encounter and what they can change.
- **References:** a small selection of inspected examples, their locations, and the particular qualities to learn from. Separate subject evidence from visual inspiration.
- **Composition:** the main object or environment, its scale in the opening view, the reading order, and the first useful control.
- **Visual language:** typography roles, palette, material or image treatment, spacing, and the relationship between interface and exhibit.
- **Signature interaction:** the action that makes this subject worth exploring interactively, plus the visible consequence.
- **Required assets:** the images, models, diagrams, sound, or data that carry the experience. Identify the essential asset to obtain or produce first.

Use this note to make decisions, then build. It is an internal working brief, not a new user approval stage. Adapt a reference's useful qualities without copying its identity, text, or restricted assets.

## Finish one representative scene

Before multiplying routes or filling a catalogue, complete an exhibit scene with its intended assets, composition, typography, motion, and main interaction. It must demonstrate the quality intended for the finished app on desktop and a narrow phone.

Review the scene at normal viewing size:

- Is the subject immediately recognizable? Can the visitor inspect meaningful detail?
- Does the composition direct attention to the object and its control?
- Are material, lighting, depth, scale, and illustration treatment internally consistent where applicable?
- Does manipulation produce a clear, subject-specific consequence?
- Does the interface have an intentional hierarchy instead of identical containers competing for attention?
- Does the phone composition preserve the same idea, with appropriate framing and readable controls?

Fix weaknesses in this scene before using it as a pattern for more content. Placeholder geometry, generic decorative shapes, and unfinished images may help construction, but they do not establish the final visual standard. Simple geometry is appropriate when it is the subject or an intentionally designed explanatory representation.

## Choose the representation deliberately

| Representation | Use when | Quality work to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2D | Maps, timelines, specimens, typography, diagrams, or comparisons communicate the relationship clearly. | Image selection, framing, annotation, scale, responsive composition, and precise interaction. |
| 2.5D | Layers, a cutaway, or restrained parallax clarify depth without a full spatial model. | Coherent layering, reveal boundaries, alignment, and readable labels. |
| 3D | Turning, assembling, lighting, viewpoint, or spatial movement materially contributes to the experience. | Appropriate models or surfaces, lighting, camera behavior, attachment of labels, and device workload. |

Polish does not require a particular framework or renderer. Choose the implementation needed by the exhibit. Retain a good existing stack; use the bundled reference for its decisions and interaction quality, without imposing its space theme on another subject.

## Adapt to the subject

- **Ocean:** compose a depth journey or habitat scene around sourced organisms and a readable change in conditions. Use layered illustration or photography when it explains more than a generic underwater camera flight.
- **Dinosaurs:** make specimen detail, anatomy, or scale compelling. A carefully composed fossil inspection or skeletal comparison can carry the exhibit; distinguish the fossil from reconstructed appearance.
- **Cultures:** center named people, places, objects, and contemporary context. Let credited photographs, maker accounts, maps, and object detail establish the identity. Avoid a universal national-symbol card collection.
- **Machines:** show the mechanism working. A cutaway, assembly, or linked movement should reveal the causal relationship between parts; the surrounding historical material adds context.
- **Periodic table:** use the table's structure, material imagery, and focused transformations. Give families, properties, or particle counts a coherent visual explanation rather than decorating every cell with an unrelated effect.

Collections and guided activities can extend a strong exhibit. Choose their number and presentation from the subject and request; neither a three-card homepage nor three quizzes is a default requirement.
