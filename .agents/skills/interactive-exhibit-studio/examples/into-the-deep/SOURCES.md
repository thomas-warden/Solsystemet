## Scope and accuracy decisions

- The three stops — green sea turtle at **about 20 m**, vampire squid at **about 700 m**, and dumbo octopus at **about 3,000 m** — are illustrative expedition locations. They are **not** exact habitat limits, guaranteed sightings, or a claim that all three animals occur on one continuous local transect.
- Use **sunlit ocean**, **twilight zone**, and **midnight zone** for these stops. The midnight zone is approximately **1,000–4,000 m**. All water below 1,000 m is not called the midnight zone: abyssal and hadal zones lie deeper.
- Vampire squid feed mostly on marine snow. Avoid absolute wording such as “never eats animals”: the aquarium notes occasional animal prey in older, larger individuals.
- Vampire squid eyes can look blue when reflecting research lights. **The eyes should not be portrayed as independently glowing.** The arm tips can emit blue bioluminescence.
- The dumbo is **Grimpoteuthis**, a genus containing multiple species. Avoid implying a single species or that every member shares one exact size or depth range. Its “ears” are **fins**, with webbing between its eight arms.
- Label artwork as **“Illustration · animals shown with light · sizes not to scale”** or comparable clear wording. Lighting an animal for visibility is an illustration choice, not a claim that sunlight reaches 3,000 m.
- The proposed “vampire squid 600–900 m” range was **not independently verified** on the accessible authoritative pages below. The aquarium documents a photograph deeper than 600 m and the animal's deep, low-oxygen habitat. The 700 m illustration is suitable as an approximate stop, but do not publish 600–900 m as its complete range on this evidence.
- Common octopus was considered during research but is not one of the selected stops. No exact shallow-water common-octopus claim is supported by this source set; do not interchange a common octopus, two-spot octopus, and dumbo octopus.

## Claim-to-source map

### Ocean light and depth zones

**NOAA Ocean Exploration — How far does light travel in the ocean?**

<https://oceanexplorer.noaa.gov/ocean-fact/light-distributed/>

Verified claims:

- Sunlight zone: upper 200 m.
- Twilight zone: about 200–1,000 m, with much less light.
- No sunlight penetrates below about 1,000 m.
- Midnight/bathypelagic zone: about 1,000–4,000 m.

Supporting excerpts:

> “The upper 200 meters (656 feet) of the ocean is called the euphotic zone.”

> “The area between 200 and 1,000 meters (656 and 3,280 feet) is the dysphotic zone.”

> “Sunlight does not penetrate the eternal darkness below 1,000 meters (3,280 feet)”

> “midnight zone (or bathypelagic zone) between 1,000 and 4,000 meters”

**NOAA National Ocean Service — How far does light travel in the ocean?**

<https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/light_travel.html>

Corroborates the divisions and the educational simplification. The diagram transcript says:

> “The second division from 200 meters to 1,000 meters is labeled as the dysphotic (twilight) zone”

> “photosynthesis is not possible here.”

Suggested child-facing wording: “Sunlight fades as we dive. Below about 1,000 metres, no sunlight reaches us.” Numerical boundaries are broad ocean-zone conventions; local water clarity varies.

### Green sea turtle

**NOAA Fisheries — Green Turtle**

<https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/green-turtle>

Verified claims:

- Green sea turtles are reptiles that breathe air and must return to the surface.
- Growing juveniles move into shallow coastal foraging areas; adults commonly use these coastal habitats.
- They mainly eat algae and seagrasses, with qualifications by life stage and population.
- No exact 20 m habitat boundary is claimed by this source.

Supporting excerpts:

> “Green turtles, like all sea turtles, are reptiles and must surface to breathe.”

> “Juveniles eventually leave the open ocean habitat and travel to nearshore foraging grounds in shallow coastal habitats”

> “Their diet mainly consists of algae and seagrasses”

Suggested child-facing wording: “I graze in shallow coastal waters, but I must swim to the surface to breathe air.”

### Vampire squid

**Monterey Bay Aquarium — Vampire squid**

<https://www.montereybayaquarium.org/animals-the-ocean/animals-a-to-z/vampire-squid>

Verified claims:

- Scientific name: *Vampyroteuthis infernalis*.
- A distinct cephalopod, not a true squid; it is more closely related to octopuses.
- Lives in dark, low-oxygen deep water, most often the oxygen-minimum zone.
- Collects drifting marine snow with long feeding filaments.
- Arm-tip organs produce blue bioluminescence; blue-looking eyes reflect light from a remotely operated vehicle.
- The page captions a photograph taken deeper than 600 m.

Supporting excerpts:

> “the vampire squid is most often found in the oxygen-minimum zone.”

> “Mostly marine snow—particles of dead animals, rotting materials, poop, and snot.”

> “To feed, the vampire squid extends its two thin filaments covered in adhesive mucus.”

> “A light-producing organ produces bright blue bioluminescence at each tip of a vampire squid’s arms.”

> “when illuminated by the light of a remotely operated vehicle they reflect the color of the surrounding water and appear blue.”

> “photographed this vampire squid with arms spread wide at more than 600 meters (2,000 feet) deep.”

Suggested child-facing wording: “My spooky name is a surprise: I mostly collect drifting scraps called marine snow with two sticky feeding threads.”

**Smithsonian Ocean — The Vampire Squid from Hell**

<https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/vampire-squid-hell>

Corroborating excerpt:

> “collects and eats drifting particles called ‘marine snow’ using two long, sticky filaments.”

The page describes its “slow lifestyle in dark, low-oxygen water with few predators.”

**Smithsonian Ocean — Octopuses, Squids, and Relatives**

<https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/octopuses-squids-and-relatives>

Corroborating excerpt:

> “Contrary to its name, the vampire squid is actually more closely related to the octopuses.”

### Dumbo octopus

**Smithsonian Ocean — Grimpoteuthis, The Dumbo Octopus**

<https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/grimpoteuthis-dumbo-octopus>

Verified claims:

- Deep-sea *Grimpoteuthis* occur around the planned 3,000 m stop.
- The source gives 9,800–13,000 ft, approximately 2,987–3,962 m. In brief prose, “around 3,000–4,000 m” is a rounded conversion of this source, not a definitive range for every species.
- They have mantle fins and webbed arms.
- They often hover just above the seabed and look for food such as snails and worms.

Supporting excerpts:

> “lives on the ocean floor at extreme depths of 9,800 to 13,000 feet.”

> “a pair of fins located on their mantle—their namesake—and webbing between their arms.”

> “hover just above the seafloor looking for snails, worms, and other food.”

**Smithsonian Ocean — A Dumbo Octopus with Curly Arms**

<https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/dumbo-octopus-curly-arms>

Supporting excerpt:

> “the dumbo octopus uses its ear-like fins to swim in the water column.”

Suggested child-facing wording: “These ‘ears’ are fins! I flap them to swim through the deep ocean.”

### Bioluminescence

**NOAA National Ocean Service — What is bioluminescence?**

<https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/biolum.html>

Verified claims:

- Living things can make light through chemical reactions.
- Marine bioluminescence occurs from surface habitats to the deep sea.
- Depending on the animal, it can help deter predators, find prey, or communicate.

Supporting excerpts:

> “The light emitted by a bioluminescent organism is produced by energy released from chemical reactions occurring inside (or ejected by) the organism.”

> “Bioluminescent creatures are found throughout marine habitats, from the ocean surface to the deep seafloor.”

Suggested child-facing wording: “Some animals make their own light. This is called bioluminescence.” Do not generalize this ability to every deep-ocean animal or to the dumbo octopus.
