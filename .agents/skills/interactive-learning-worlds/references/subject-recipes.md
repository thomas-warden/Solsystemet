# Subject recipes

These are starting designs for ages 8–10, not mandatory templates or validated curricula. Choose a small, coherent scope and verify its claims before building. Each mission should take roughly 1–3 minutes, subject to actual testing. Use [learning-design.md](learning-design.md) for completion, age adaptation, and source requirements.

For every recipe, keep an accessible static representation alongside any richer rendering. Use real evidence where it helps; label reconstructed or illustrative parts. The source links below are research starting points: find the specific supporting page, record, or paper for each claim.

## Dinosaurs: evidence from a lost world

**Useful representations:** annotated fossil photographs, a geological timeline, a skeleton diagram with known and inferred bones distinguished. Add a rotatable model only when comparing shape or viewing hidden anatomy helps; a walk-through 3D landscape is not required.

**Meaningful controls:** align two tooth outlines; reveal fossil versus reconstructed parts; move a timeline window while keeping each animal's dated range visible.

| Mission | Actions and evidence | Discovery and completion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Teeth leave clues | Inspect sourced teeth from two named species; magnify their edges and compare them with a short evidence key. | Choose which observed feature supports a proposed diet. Explain that teeth provide clues, not a complete menu. Require an inspection and a supported choice. |
| Did they meet? | Place sourced date ranges for Stegosaurus and Tyrannosaurus on the same timeline. Compare the intervals, not just their order. | Identify that these selected animals lived at different times. Keep approximate dates and uncertainty visible; do not make pixel-perfect placement the learning goal. |
| Build what we know | Assemble a small section of a skeleton from a documented specimen; switch between preserved bones and an explicitly labeled reconstruction. | Identify one part supported by the specimen and one inferred part. Reward recognizing the evidence limit, not merely finishing the assembly. |

**Pitfalls and sources:** separate a species' known fossil range from the lifespan of one animal. Do not mix species, continents, or periods without explanation. Birds are living dinosaurs; “all dinosaurs became extinct” is misleading. Treat soft tissue, colors, sounds, and behavior as uncertain unless the particular claim has support. A museum mount can include casts or inferred bones. Useful starting points: [Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/), [Natural History Museum](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/), and [Paleobiology Database](https://paleobiodb.org/). Check the specimen or taxon record and the date of the interpretation.

## Periodic table: what makes an element?

**Useful representations:** a readable table, an element card, and a simple nucleus or particle-count diagram. State that the diagram is a model; decorative planetary electron orbits should not be presented as literal paths.

**Meaningful controls:** change proton count; highlight a family; compare sourced properties under stated conditions. A table can do more useful work than a room full of floating 3D atoms.

| Mission | Actions and evidence | Discovery and completion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give an atom its name | Change the proton count and watch the element name and atomic number update. Use a small range of elements. | Find a requested element from its proton count and compare it with a neighbor. Explain that proton count defines the element; atomic mass is a different quantity. |
| Find a family resemblance | Select helium, neon, and argon; reveal sourced room-temperature state and reactivity descriptions beside their shared table column. | Highlight the column and identify a shared property. Explain that these examples are usually very unreactive; do not teach “never react” as a universal rule. |
| Choose a wire material | Compare copper and aluminum cards using conductivity, mass for a stated size, and other sourced properties relevant to a stated design goal. | Choose a material and the evidence supporting that goal. Accept more than one defensible choice when there is a tradeoff; do not invent a universal best metal. |

**Pitfalls and sources:** distinguish atomic number, mass number, and standard atomic weight. Isotopes change neutron count; ions change electron count. Physical properties depend on temperature, pressure, purity, and form. Compounds and everyday objects are not single elements merely because they contain them. Do not turn chemical hazard information into unsupervised mixing instructions. Useful starting points: [IUPAC](https://iupac.org/), [Royal Society of Chemistry periodic table](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/), and [NIST reference data](https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/). Preserve units, conditions, and the data source's precision.

## Cultures: people, objects, and context

**Choose the scope first:** a named community, place, time, and publicly documented practice or object. Describe living people in the present as well as historical contexts where relevant. “Cultures of the world” is too broad for three factual missions without a carefully selected scope.

**Useful representations:** credited photographs, a map with time context, object records, and accounts from identified makers or community members. Use audio only when it has a clear purpose and appropriate provenance, with text equivalents. A 3D artifact viewer is useful when construction or multiple sides matter.

| Mission | Actions and evidence | Discovery and completion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meet the maker | Inspect one documented everyday object and its maker or community attribution, place, and date. Connect the record to its location and context. | Identify who made or used this particular object and what the record supports. Avoid guessing a nationality from an image. Preserve unknown attribution as unknown. |
| Try one technique | For a publicly documented process, manipulate a simple diagram—for example, pass virtual strips over and under to examine a weaving structure. Show the source example beside it. | Complete a small pattern and identify the structural relationship. Label the activity as a simplified demonstration; it does not confer expertise in the community's practice. |
| One community, many lives | Compare two documented objects or accounts from the chosen community. Toggle their dates, materials, or uses and inspect their explanations. | Find one supported difference or continuity. Accept variation; do not make one object the correct or authentic version of an entire culture. |

**Pitfalls and sources:** avoid national stereotypes, ranked cultures, exoticizing language, and treating communities as frozen in time. Clothing, food, language, and homes vary within communities and across time. Distinguish a creator's account, a curator's interpretation, and a historical outsider's description. Use self-identification and trusted translations; retain original names when useful. Do not invent traditional stories, quotations, accents, sacred practices, or permission to reuse restricted material. Prefer community-run institutions, named creators, and community-reviewed collections; compare historical records with contemporary community sources. Museum publication alone does not establish community agreement or unrestricted reuse rights.

## Machines through time: causes and tradeoffs

**Useful representations:** a lever diagram, linked gears with visible teeth, a cutaway, and dated drawings or photographs. Use a 2D mechanism when it makes motion and forces easier to read. Use 3D for assembly, hidden parts, or genuinely spatial relationships.

**Meaningful controls:** move a fulcrum with load held constant; turn one of two engaged gears; change flow through a clearly simplified waterwheel. Show a result tied to the mechanism rather than an arbitrary animation speed.

| Mission | Actions and evidence | Discovery and completion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lift with less force | Move a lever's fulcrum to two positions while keeping the load fixed. Compare effort force and movement distance. | Choose the position requiring less force and notice the longer effort travel. Explain the ideal model's force–distance tradeoff and omitted friction. |
| Which way will it turn? | Predict the direction of two directly meshing external gears, then rotate the first. Compare tooth counts and turns. | Correctly relate opposite rotation and the tooth-count ratio after observing a full input turn. State this mechanism's conditions; do not generalize to belts or internal gears. |
| Follow the power | Inspect a sourced historical waterwheel drawing; trace water → wheel → connected mechanism. Change one supported variable in a labeled simplified model. | Identify what supplies the motion and one design constraint supported by the record. Connect the mechanism to a particular place and time, not a universal story of progress. |

**Pitfalls and sources:** conserve the relationships the activity claims to teach; visual plausibility is insufficient. More mechanical advantage does not create energy. Label omitted friction and losses, and avoid exact historical performance figures without evidence. An ideal model is not a construction or safety specification. Historical inventions often have multiple contributors and regional histories; distinguish documented use, surviving examples, patents, and disputed priority. Useful starting points: [Science Museum](https://www.sciencemuseum.org.uk/), [Smithsonian collections](https://www.si.edu/collections), [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/), and an appropriate physics or engineering reference for the mechanism itself.

## Ocean: light, floating, and food

**Useful representations:** a depth transect, an adjustable buoyancy diagram, and a small food web for a named habitat. Use underwater 3D when depth or viewpoint contributes to the lesson; keep measurements and relationships readable without it.

**Meaningful controls:** move a depth marker through a sourced example; change a virtual object's volume at fixed mass; trace energy through a documented food web. Keep environmental conditions explicit.

| Mission | Actions and evidence | Discovery and completion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Follow the fading light | Move down a depth transect and compare the light reading or qualitative bands at two depths. Use a specified location or clearly labeled typical illustration. | Identify where light becomes insufficient for the photosynthesis shown in this example. Explain that clear and cloudy water differ; avoid a single universal cutoff. |
| Make it float | Keep a virtual object's mass fixed while changing its volume in water with stated conditions. Compare floating and sinking results. | Produce and compare both outcomes, then connect them to average density and displaced water. Explain this freely floating model's limits; do not imply size alone decides floating. |
| Trace a meal's energy | Explore a documented surface-water food web. Select connected organisms and follow arrows explicitly labeled as energy moving from food to eater. | Trace one valid path from a primary producer to a consumer and recognize an alternative where supported. Distinguish this habitat from deep-sea systems supported by chemosynthesis. |

**Pitfalls and sources:** depth zones are useful approximations, not sharp global walls. Temperature, pressure, salinity, oxygen, and light vary independently and by location. Species do not occupy one fixed depth everywhere. Changes to one food-web node do not justify a certain numerical ecosystem forecast. Distinguish the ocean floor from water depth and a local measurement from a global average. Useful starting points: [NOAA Ocean Service](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/), [MBARI](https://www.mbari.org/), and [Smithsonian Ocean](https://ocean.si.edu/). Link measurements to their place, time, method, and units.

## Adapting a recipe

Keep the learning objective when changing the presentation. For an emerging reader, reduce visible choices and use a labeled comparison; for an older learner, reveal numerical data, competing evidence, or model limitations. Do not merely add harder vocabulary. Three missions are an initial product scope, not a requirement to stretch weak evidence into a third lesson.
