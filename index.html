About the gold pointer & this model

The gold pointer is a drawn guide. The Moon turns once per orbit, keeping that side toward Earth. The guide hides when blocked or seen end-on.

Body sizes are proportional; distance is shortened. Earth’s daily spin is omitted. Sunlight is illustrative, not today’s sky or a phase calendar. Sources & model notes ↗

SOLSYSTEMET / FIELD NOTES

Light makes a world.

Explore eight planets and twelve moons with sunlight, surface detail and changing perspective. The imagery comes from observations; the movement and lighting are an interactive reconstruction.

Earth

NASA’s Blue Marble July 2004 surface composite, with a separate cloud layer and city lights. The cloud layer moves independently and casts shadows. Local solar time follows longitude and the same Sun direction used by the renderer; it is not civil clock time. The model has no seasons.

Jupiter & Saturn

Jupiter uses spacecraft imagery. Cloud drift is illustrative, not a measured wind simulation. Saturn uses a mapped, gently banded atmosphere and a separate ring plane, with both ring-on-planet and planet-on-ring shadows. Each world is framed separately, not at a common scale.

The orrery

One clock drives all eight elliptical orbits, using fixed J2000 NASA/JPL elements and orbital periods. Starting positions are illustrative, not the current sky. Easy-to-see spacing spreads the planets for clarity. Real distances uses the same distance scale for every orbit; bodies remain enlarged as navigational markers. The one-year experiment stops automatically after one Earth orbit.

Moons & small worlds

Visit Earth’s Moon, the four Galilean moons of Jupiter and seven major moons of Saturn. Their orbit views use measured periods, circular paths and enlarged bodies. The asteroid belt has an imagined fly-through with generated rock shapes and compressed spacing. Its orrery view uses a small illustrative sample; individual rocks are exaggerated so you can see the band. Uranus’s gold dot and lines are drawn teaching guides, not atmospheric features.

The sky beyond

The pale band behind the worlds is the Milky Way, our home galaxy. NASA’s catalogue-based star map gives it real stars and dark dust lanes. We brighten the sky so you can see it beside the sunlit planets. Sky credits & notes ↗

Motion & performance

Each world starts rotating when you arrive. Pause stops rotation and cloud drift. Orbital scenes and asteroid flight also start moving on arrival. Drag or use arrow keys over the scene to inspect a world. Reduced motion is respected. If the graphics engine is unavailable, a still illustration is shown and simulation controls are disabled.
